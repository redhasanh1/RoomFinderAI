# The thing that decides whether this is legal is the SOURCE of the lead

Not whether we scrape or hand-type. Not whether we personalise. **Where the
landlord's contact details came from.** That single distinction separates a
safe campaign from a ruinous one.

## The precedent that is this exact plan

**craigslist v. RadPad.** A startup scraped craigslist rental listings and
emailed those landlords to recruit them onto a competing rental platform.
Same business model, same tactic, same target.

**$60.5M consent judgment.**

That is the plan as originally described, already litigated to conclusion.

## Why "I'll do it manually through the browser" does not fix it

Doing it by hand solves the *automation* clause. It does not touch:

- **Rentals.ca §3.8** and **RentFaster** (identical template) — express
  **"Prohibition on Solicitation"** covering precisely this use.
- **Zumper §11** — bans using the site to promote a competitive service, or
  to *"solicit, divert or take away... prospective customers of Zumper."*
- **Craigslist** — prices the breach in advance: **$25 per unsolicited
  email, $500 per text.** Liquidated damages, no argument about harm needed.
- **Kijiji** — ToS governed by **Ontario law with submission to Ontario
  courts**. The one platform that can sue Hasan at home with no forum fight.

## PIPEDA s.7.1(2) — the sharper edge, and it bites before we send anything

Inserted by CASL. Collecting addresses using a program **designed primarily
to harvest them** switches off every without-consent exception, including
Schedule 1 cl. 4.3.

The collection is unlawful **the moment it happens** — before a single
message is sent — and **cannot be cured retroactively**. There is no
"we only emailed the good ones" defence.

## CASL details that catch people who think they are compliant

- **s.10(9)(b) conspicuous-publication implied consent fails at step one
  wherever the platform shows a proxy/relay address** — which is most of
  Craigslist and much of Kijiji. The landlord never published that address;
  the platform minted it. No publication, no implied consent.
- **Implied consent only satisfies s.6(1)(a).** The s.6(2) duties still
  apply in full: sender identification, contact information valid for
  **60 days**, and a working unsubscribe. Founders routinely think implied
  consent means "no formalities". It does not.
- **s.13** puts the **burden of proving consent on the sender.** If we
  cannot document why we believed we had consent for a given address, we
  have no defence.
- **s.20(4)** penalties: up to **$1M** for an individual, $10M for a
  business.

## Where leads CAN come from — no ToS, no liquidated damages

These carry no platform contract and no solicitation clause:

1. **Municipal rental-licensing registries.** Several Ontario municipalities
   licence rental housing and publish registries. Public record, no ToS.
2. **University and college off-campus housing offices.** They exist to
   connect landlords with students. We are a resource, not a solicitor.
   Highest leverage of all — one relationship, many landlords.
3. **Landlord associations.** Vendor partnerships, newsletters, events —
   the sanctioned way to reach a membership.
4. **Referrals** from landlords already on the platform.
5. **Inbound**: SEO, content, paid ads. The landlord contacts us; consent is
   express and documented.
6. **In person.** Campus boards, community boards, events.
7. **Our own users.** Anyone who signed up has an existing business
   relationship under CASL.

## What this means for the campaign

The outreach templates stay — the copy is fine and the market angle is
right. What changes is **who we send them to**. We do not harvest Kijiji,
Craigslist, Rentals.ca or Zumper. We go to registries, campuses,
associations and inbound.

That is slower per contact and far better per contact: a landlord reached
through their university's housing office arrives with borrowed trust, and
nobody can sue us for it.
